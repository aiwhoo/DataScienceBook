Data Visualization

Part 2: Turning Data into Visuals

Author

Technical Instructional Team

Why Visualize Data?

A table full of numbers can be hard to interpret. A clear chart, however, can reveal the same pattern in seconds.

Data visualization is not just about making information look good. It is about making information clear, memorable, and useful.

Good visuals help us:

  • Spot patterns that are hard to notice in raw numbers
  • Save time by making the main idea easier to grasp
  • Tell a story by turning data into a message people can understand

The Chart Selection Rule

One of the most common mistakes in data visualization is choosing the wrong chart type.

A simple rule helps: start with your question. Ask yourself what you are trying to show, then choose the chart that communicates that idea most clearly.

NoteGolden Rule

Choose the chart that answers your question most clearly—not the one that looks the most impressive.

The Bar Chart

A bar chart is best for comparing values across categories.

Examples include:

  • Test scores across different classes
  • Sales for different products
  • Population by city or state
  • Wins across sports teams

A bar chart makes differences easy to compare because each bar represents a quantity.

TipBest Use

Think compare → think bar chart.

The Line Chart

A line chart is used to show how something changes over time.

Examples include:

  • GPA across several semesters
  • Daily temperature over a week
  • Social media followers growing month by month

Line charts are especially useful for showing trends, increases, decreases, and patterns across time.

TipBest Use

Think trend or over time → think line chart.

The Pie Chart

A pie chart shows how categories make up a whole.

It works best when:

  • There are five or fewer categories
  • The values add up to 100%
  • The slices are clearly different in size

Pie charts can be helpful, but they are often overused.

Avoid them when:

  • You need to compare exact values
  • You have too many categories
  • The slices are very similar in size

In many cases, a bar chart is easier to read.

The Scatter Plot

A scatter plot shows the relationship between two numerical variables.

Examples include:

  • Hours studied vs. exam score
  • Temperature vs. ice cream sales
  • Height vs. shoe size
  • Sleep hours vs. mood rating

Scatter plots help us look for relationships or correlations between variables.

If the dots rise together, the relationship may be positive. If they fall, it may be negative. If the dots are scattered randomly, there may be no clear pattern.

NoteCorrelation Reminder

Correlation is not the same as causation. A relationship between two variables does not automatically mean one causes the other.

Common Mistakes in Data Visualization

Even good data can be misunderstood if the chart is poorly designed.

Some common mistakes include:

Wrong Chart Type

Using a line chart for unrelated categories or a pie chart for trends over time can confuse the reader.

Too Much Data

Crowding too many categories into one chart creates visual clutter and makes the message harder to see.

Misleading Scales

Changing the axis scale can exaggerate or hide differences. Honest scales help people trust what they see.

ImportantWhy Design Choices Matter

A misleading chart can change how people understand reality. Clear visualization is part of responsible data analysis.

Comparing Multiple Variables

Sometimes one chart is not enough. When your data involves several variables at once, you may need more advanced visual strategies.

Examples include:

  • Grouped bar charts to compare several categories within each group
  • Multi-line charts to compare trends across groups over time
  • Color or size encoding in a scatter plot to add more layers of information

These visuals can be powerful, but they should still remain easy to read.

Activity: Which Chart Would You Use?

Choose the best chart type for each scenario:

  • Showing how school lunch participation changed each month
  • Showing the percentage of students who prefer each school subject
  • Exploring the relationship between sleep and GPA
  • Comparing wins across five sports teams

Possible chart choices include:

  • Bar chart
  • Line chart
  • Pie chart
  • Scatter plot
TipGoal of the Activity

Do not just name a chart—explain why it fits the question.
