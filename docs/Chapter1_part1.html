Chapter 1: What is Data?

Part 1: Understanding the Invisible Footprints of Our Lives

Author

Technical Instructional Team

Every action leaves a trace. Learning to notice and interpret those traces is one of the first steps in data science.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Define data and explain why it matters in everyday life.
  • Describe the basic lifecycle of data: collected, stored, and analyzed.
  • Distinguish between structured and unstructured data.
  • Investigate how personal digital activity creates measurable patterns.
  • Reflect critically on the limits, gaps, and possible biases in data.

The Big Question

We hear the word data all the time, but what does it actually mean?

Data is more than numbers on a screen. It includes the traces left behind by the things we do every day.

ImportantThink About It

In the last 24 hours, how many digital traces have you left behind?

Data as Invisible Footprints

A useful way to think about data is to imagine it as invisible footprints.

Just as footprints in wet ground can reveal where someone walked, digital footprints can reveal patterns about how we live.

  • Clicking: Every tap, swipe, or click can be recorded.
  • Searching: Every search can reveal a question, need, or curiosity.
  • Walking with your phone: Your location may be tracked over time.
  • Streaming and messaging: Your choices and communication habits create records.
NoteKey Idea

You do not need to be doing something “technical” to create data. Ordinary life already produces a constant stream of information.

The Big Answer

At its core, data is information generated by actions, observations, and interactions.

A single data point may not say much on its own. But when many data points are combined, patterns begin to emerge.

For example:

  • Step counts can reveal activity level and movement patterns.
  • Music history can reflect habits, preferences, and moods.
  • Location data can show routines and regular destinations.
  • Text messages can reveal patterns in communication and tone.

The Lifecycle of Data

Data does not simply appear and disappear. It usually moves through a process.

Collected

Data is gathered from phones, apps, websites, sensors, cameras, and other tools.

Stored

That information is kept on devices, in apps, on servers, or in the cloud.

Analyzed

People and computers study the data to find patterns, answer questions, or make decisions.

TipInstructor Insight

One of the most important ideas in data science is that data becomes useful when it is interpreted. Raw information matters, but insight comes from asking good questions.

Why Data Matters

Data matters because it helps people understand what is happening and decide what to do next.

In Business

Companies use data to understand customers, improve products, reduce costs, and make better decisions.

In Technology

Apps, recommendations, notifications, and smart tools all rely on data.

In the Wider World

Data helps people study climate change, public health, education, transportation, and many other real-world problems.

NoteBig Picture

Data is not just about computers. It is about understanding people, behavior, systems, and the world around us.

Two Main Types of Data

Not all data looks the same. A helpful starting point in data science is to separate it into two broad categories: structured and unstructured data.

Structured Data

Structured data is organized, consistent, and easy to sort or analyze.

Examples include:

  • Step counts
  • GPS coordinates
  • Calendar events
  • Survey responses in rows and columns

Unstructured Data

Unstructured data does not fit neatly into a table. It is often messier, but also richer in meaning and context.

Examples include:

  • Photos
  • Videos
  • Audio recordings
  • Text messages
  • Social media posts
NoteStructured vs. Unstructured

Structured data is often easier for computers to process quickly.

Unstructured data is harder to organize, but it often captures emotion, context, and real-life complexity.

Why Both Types Matter

Each type of data has strengths.

  • Structured data makes it easier to count, compare, filter, and graph information.
  • Unstructured data helps us understand meaning, tone, context, and human experience.

In real-world data science, both are important. Together, they provide a fuller picture of what is happening.
